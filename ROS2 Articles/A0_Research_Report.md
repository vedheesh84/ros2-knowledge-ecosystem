Distributed Systems Architecture in Autonomous Robotics: First-Principles Foundations of ROS 2Robotic software systems diverge fundamentally from enterprise, cloud, and web application architectures because a physical robot is not a central computer orchestrating passive peripherals; it is an embodied, distributed cyber-physical network operating under non-deterministic physical conditions. Where enterprise software relies on centralized database brokers, persistent connectivity, and synchronous request-response interfaces such as HTTP/REST, physical robots operate amidst actuator saturation, sensor packet loss, variable compute latencies, and intermittent radio communications. Structuring robotics software around centralized coordination creates single points of failure, unmanageable latency spikes, and catastrophic mechanical damage when a central coordinator stalls or crashes.The architectural foundations of the Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) are the direct mathematical and systems-level consequences of solving real-time perception, planning, and control within uncertain physical environments. ROS 2 replaces centralized daemon registries with a decentralized, brokerless data-centric publish-subscribe architecture built on the Object Management Group (OMG) Data Distribution Service (DDS) and Real-Time Publish-Subscribe (RTPS) standards. By delegating peer discovery, state serialization, and Quality of Service (QoS) enforcement to an abstracted middleware layer, ROS 2 enables sovereign software nodes to execute as isolated processes that exchange telemetry and commands asynchronously.Mastering ROS 2 requires internalizing this distributed systems model before inspecting application programming interfaces or writing package manifests. Every software node must function as an autonomous agent operating strictly on partial, localized knowledge, tolerating transmission delays, dropped messages, and peer terminations gracefully. Understanding the mechanical properties of this substrate—from operating system socket buffers and RTPS endpoint discovery to single-threaded executor scheduling anomalies and 802.11 wireless multicast degradation—provides the engineering foundation necessary to design, integrate, and debug production-grade autonomous robots.1. Article ThesisArticle A0 ("Distributed Systems Thinking: Why Robots Must Work Alone Together") establishes that decentralization in robotics software is an operational necessity driven by physical laws rather than a stylistic architectural preference. The primary learning objective is to dismantle the intuition of centralized software design—in which a single executive loop queries inputs, computes state, and issues commands—and replace it with a decentralized mental model where independent agents coordinate through localized state exchange.The article demonstrates that dynamic environments, intermittent wireless connectivity, and real-time operational deadlines make centralized coordination inherently fragile. By examining the biological self-organization of a beehive alongside the failure modes of centralized dispatch systems, Article A0 shows that complex, adaptive macroscopic behaviors emerge from microscopic interactions among autonomous agents acting without a coordinating executive. The core pedagogical progression teaches that the primary interaction patterns in robotics—continuous broadcast streaming, reliable point-to-point queries, and preemptable long-running tasks—are the three essential coordination primitives required to prevent deadlock in multi-rate cyber-physical systems.Article A0 assumes basic computer science literacy, including familiarity with processes, threads, networks, and client-server concepts, while assuming no prior exposure to robotics frameworks, DDS, or control theory. To keep the conceptual focus clear, the article intentionally abstracts away the operational details of dynamic network discovery, data serialization, thread scheduling, and kernel socket transport. Moving beyond this introductory model requires translating these biological analogies into concrete systems engineering: analyzing how the fallacies of distributed computing impact physical hardware, how brokerless discovery operates across network layers, and how to structure real-time perception-actuation pipelines to achieve determinism, safety, and fault isolation.2. Knowledge LadderUnderstanding distributed robotics requires progressing through seven conceptual tiers, from basic intuition to cutting-edge real-time middleware implementations:Level 1 — IntuitionA robot is an embodied collection of physical subsystems (cameras, laser scanners, motor drives, path planners) operating at widely disparate temporal frequencies. If every component must route through a central coordinator, the slowest component bottlenecks the entire machine, and any single hardware or software crash results in a catastrophic collision. Biological systems thrive because reflexes occur locally within the spinal cord while abstract decisions occur asynchronously within the cerebral cortex; robots must be constructed around this same decentralized autonomy.Level 2 — ROS 2 FundamentalsIn ROS 2, computation is distributed across independent executable processes known as Nodes. Nodes discover each other dynamically without an intermediary master daemon (such as the ROS 1 roscore), constructing a logical Computational Graph. Data exchanges map directly to the three coordination patterns: Topics provide continuous publish-subscribe streaming, Services provide reliable point-to-point request-response transactions, and Actions provide preemptable goal tracking with feedback. Command-line introspection tools (ros2 node list, ros2 topic echo, ros2 service call) permit real-time observation of this graph.Level 3 — Internal MechanicsBeneath the ROS 2 Client Library (rclcpp/rclpy) resides the ROS Middleware Interface (rmw), abstracting implementations of the OMG Data Distribution Service (DDS). Nodes instantiate DDS DomainParticipants that communicate peer-to-peer via the Real-Time Publish-Subscribe (RTPS) protocol over UDP/IP or inter-process shared memory (SHM). Dynamic participant discovery occurs via the Simple Participant Discovery Protocol (SPDP), while publisher-subscriber endpoint pairing is negotiated by the Simple Endpoint Discovery Protocol (SEDP). Data serialization occurs through the Common Data Representation (CDR) wire format. Inside the node process, an Executor polls DDS wait-sets to schedule callback functions out of ready queues onto one or more operating system threads.Level 4 — Engineering RealitiesBuilding distributed systems introduces severe operational trade-offs. Dynamic discovery over 802.11 wireless links generates quadratic ($O(N^2)$) multicast traffic that causes network flooding, packet loss, and connection dropouts. Incompatible Quality of Service (QoS) policies (such as a BEST_EFFORT publisher attempting to pair with a RELIABLE subscriber) result in silent communication failure without diagnostic errors. Furthermore, single-threaded executor architectures suffer from structural priority inversion: a slow subscriber callback or blocking service invocation prevents all other callbacks (including high-rate sensor reads or watchdog timers) from executing, inducing system-level starvation.Level 5 — Professional Robotics PracticeProduction systems partition compute across heterogeneous hardware tiers. Hard real-time control (such as 1 kHz motor torque loops and emergency-stop interlocks) executes on microcontrollers or real-time operating systems (FreeRTOS, RTEMS, Linux with PREEMPT_RT) using deterministic fieldbuses (CANopen, EtherCAT) or ros2_control. High-level autonomy (SLAM, 3D perception, Nav2 path planning, MoveIt 2 manipulation) executes on multicore industrial PCs running Linux, interacting through isolated DDS domains, discovery servers, or shared memory transports. Degradation paths are explicitly engineered: if the perception node stalls, the local motor controller continues executing a safe deceleration curve without central intervention.Level 6 — Advanced ResearchAcademic and industrial research evaluates the formal determinism of distributed processing chains. Casini et al. (2019) demonstrated that ROS 2's default executor callback scheduling mechanism fails classical real-time assumptions, necessitating Response-Time Analysis (RTA) under reservation-based and priority-driven schedulers to bound worst-case end-to-end latencies across directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). Multi-robot distributed systems apply formal consensus algorithms (such as the Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm, or CBBA) and Covariance Intersection to perform decentralized state estimation and task allocation across dynamic communication topologies subject to packet loss and network partitioning.Level 7 — Cutting EdgeThe state of the art addresses the overhead of heavy DDS implementations on modern edge robotics. The ROS 2 community has developed alternative RMW layers, most notably rmw_zenoh, which replaces heavy RTPS multicast dynamic discovery with the ultra-lightweight, zero-overhead Eclipse Zenoh protocol. Zenoh scales edge-to-cloud and multi-robot fleet communications across variable-quality networks. Concurrently, micro-ROS natively embeds DDS-XRCE (eXtremely Resource Constrained Environments) onto bare-metal microcontrollers, while integrations with IEEE 802.1 Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) bring deterministic, sub-microsecond latency bounds to distributed Ethernet backbones.4. Deep Concept ExpansionThe Beehive Paradigm and Mathematical Formalization of Local SovereigntyThe core biological analogy in Article A0 demonstrates how complex, macroscopic system order emerges from localized microscopic interaction rules. In distributed systems theory, this is formalized through agent-based models and decentralized consensus. In a centralized architecture, an orchestrator maintains global state knowledge:
$$S(t) = \{s_1(t), s_2(t), \dots, s_N(t)\}$$
The orchestrator solves an optimization problem across all states and dispatches individual control vectors $u_i(t)$ to each subsystem. As the number of nodes $N$ expands, the orchestrator's computational complexity scales nonlinearly (often $O(N^2)$ to $O(N^3)$), while network transport complexity concentrates all bandwidth demands onto the central network interface. If the coordinator is delayed by even a few milliseconds, every subsystem starves for commands.Under local sovereignty, each node $i$ maintains only its internal state $x_i$ and reads local environmental observations $y_i$. Inter-node communication is limited to nearest-neighbor state sharing over an adjacency graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$. Global coordination emerges via decentralized consensus protocols:
$$\dot{x}_i(t) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} a_{ij} (x_j(t) - x_i(t))$$
Because each node independently integrates its own control law, the loss of an adjacent node changes the local neighborhood $\mathcal{N}_i$ without halting the execution of surviving nodes. Communication bandwidth scales with the local node degree rather than the total fleet size, and communication latency impacts only the rate of system convergence rather than the immediate stability of individual local reflex loops.The Fallacies of Distributed Computing in Cyber-Physical SystemsStandard software architectures were developed under assumptions formalized and subsequently debunked by L. Peter Deutsch in the "Eight Fallacies of Distributed Computing". While these fallacies introduce minor performance penalties in enterprise IT, their consequences in cyber-physical robotics are critical to system survival:The first fallacy assumes the network is reliable. Robots operate across 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz industrial Wi-Fi characterized by multipath interference, radio-frequency shielding from metal machinery, and Doppler shift. Packet loss routinely reaches 10% to 20% in dynamic industrial facilities. Software stacks that expect reliable transport drop into recovery loops precisely when rapid maneuvering is required.The second fallacy assumes latency is zero. Operating a 100 Hz closed-loop position controller requires a bounded total transport-and-compute latency budget of under 10 milliseconds. Network round-trips over standard TCP stacks fluctuate between 2 ms and 250 ms due to retransmission timeouts, introducing phase lag that destabilizes control loops and risks mechanical oscillation.The third fallacy assumes bandwidth is infinite. Autonomous machines generate high-volume data streams: 4K RGB cameras produce raw streams requiring gigabits per second, while 3D LiDAR arrays produce point clouds consuming tens of megabytes per second. Attempting to route these uncompressed perception streams across wireless links quickly saturates the transmission medium, starving safety-critical control channels.The fourth fallacy assumes network topology is static. Autonomous mobile robots traverse physical facilities, transitioning across wireless access points through BSSID roaming or entering structural dead zones. Network addresses may shift, and links sever without warning. If software entities depend on persistent point-to-point connections, whole sub-graphs stall. Robotic middleware must therefore assume by default that the physical network is fragile, transport latency is unbounded, and peer nodes can drop without terminating their sockets cleanly.Mechanical Dissection of the Three Coordination PrimitivesROS 2 formalizes the three interaction patterns outlined in Article A0 into distinct programmatic interfaces, each engineered to address specific physical transport and timing requirements:Coordination PrimitiveUnderlying Transport ArchitectureTemporal SemanticsFailure Behavior & Edge DynamicsPrimary Robotics Use CaseTopics (Broadcast & Listen)Unidirectional DDS DataWriter to DataReader via UDP or Shared Memory.Asynchronous, non-blocking for both publisher and subscriber.Messages dropped if queues fill; consumers read latest available sample or process history buffers.Continuous high-rate sensory telemetry (IMU at 500 Hz, LiDAR at 20 Hz, Cameras at 30 Hz).Services (Request & Answer)Correlated dual-topic request-reply exchange over DDS.Synchronous or deferred asynchronous client execution; blocking server callback.Client times out if server hangs; single-threaded callers deadlock if waiting synchronously on self.Infrequent transactional queries, hardware configuration, and map triggers.Actions (Goal & Feedback)Composite structure: 3 DDS topics (goal, feedback, status) and 2 DDS services (cancel, result).Fully asynchronous state machine with continuous feedback and client preemption.Server can reject goals; client can abort; network loss triggers configurable goal expiration.Long-duration, multi-second tasks (Nav2 navigation paths, MoveIt 2 trajectory tracking).Topics are optimized for continuous data flow where data freshness takes precedence over historical completeness. Conceptual RelationshipsUnderstanding how distributed systems concepts relate, depend on, and bound one another prevents fundamental architectural errors during robotics system integration.The Architectural Dependency PipelineThe conceptual dependencies of a distributed robotic system advance in a distinct hierarchical progression:Decentralized Distributed Architecture: The foundational substrate wherein computation is partitioned across independent hardware boards, operating system processes, and network nodes.Local Sovereignty & Autonomy: Directly enabled by decentralization; requires that each node possesses complete authority over its execution cycle and local reflexes.Local Knowledge Boundaries: The informational constraint of local autonomy; nodes maintain state derived strictly from immediate sensors and local neighborhoods, preventing global data bottlenecks.Asynchronous Execution: The operational mechanism enforcing local sovereignty; computational threads dispatch and receive data without blocking on peer state.Brokerless Peer-to-Peer Discovery: The networking layer required to eliminate single points of failure, enabling nodes to dynamically resolve endpoints via RTPS without a central daemon.The Three Coordination Primitives (Topics, Services, Actions): The functional interfaces built atop the brokerless asynchronous substrate, providing the appropriate semantic guarantees for continuous streaming, transactional querying, and preemptable task execution.Critical Engineering Boundaries and DistinctionsConfusing related abstractions leads to structural bugs during system design:Decentralized Topology versus Uncoordinated Systems represents a common boundary error. The absence of a central master does not imply that software nodes act without alignment. Coordination in a decentralized system is achieved through strongly typed interface contracts (such as ROS 2 message, service, and action specifications), deterministic state machines, and consensus protocols rather than centralized oversight.A ROS 2 Node is not identical to an Operating System Process. A node is a logical unit of computation within the computational graph. Multiple ROS 2 nodes can be instantiated within a single operating system process container as Component Nodes. This preserves logical modularity while allowing intra-process shared memory transports to bypass the network stack entirely, executing zero-copy pointer exchanges.Data-Centricity differs fundamentally from Traditional Message Passing. In message-oriented middleware, communication channels are opaque pipes that route raw byte buffers between specific network addresses. In data-centric middleware like DDS, the infrastructure understands the data types, internal data structures, and state history. DDS maintains a logically unified Global Data Space where nodes publish changes of state, and the middleware ensures that state is automatically synchronized according to fine-grained QoS policies.Best-Effort Delivery must not be equated with Defective Software. In enterprise software, packet loss is treated as an exceptional error condition. In real-time robotics, best-effort transport is a deliberate strategy to ensure latency bounds. Dropping an out-of-date sensor packet to deliver current data immediately maintains the phase margin of a feedback loop, whereas waiting for retransmissions introduces unbounded delays that degrade system stability.DimensionMechanism AMechanism BBoundary Line & Trade-OffDiscovery ArchitectureCentralized Registry (ROS 1 roscore / DNS).Brokerless Discovery (ROS 2 DDS RTPS).Centralized registries generate linear $O(N)$ discovery traffic but create a single point of failure; brokerless dynamic discovery ensures complete fault isolation but generates quadratic $O(N^2)$ discovery traffic over the network.Execution CouplingSynchronous RPC (ROS 2 Services).Asynchronous Streams (ROS 2 Topics).Services provide certainty of reception at the expense of blocking caller threads; Topics guarantee non-blocking asynchronous execution at the expense of delivery certainty.State ScopeGlobal State Aggregation.Local Knowledge Boundaries.Global state eliminates uncertainty but incurs unmanageable bandwidth and computational synchronization latency; Local state ensures microsecond reaction speeds but requires algorithms that handle partial observability.6. The User Application Layer defines robotics algorithms inside classes inheriting from rclcpp::Node or rclpy.node.Node. Nodes declare typed publishers, subscriptions, service servers, and action clients.The ROS Client Library Layer (rclcpp/rclpy) manages node lifecycles, timer callbacks, parameters, and executor wait-sets. It sits atop rcl (the common ROS client library written in C), which standardizes operational logic across language bindings.The ROS Middleware Interface (rmw) is a standardized C-based abstraction layer. It shields client libraries from vendor-specific DDS APIs, translating standard ROS types and graph events into operations compatible with eProsima Fast DDS, Eclipse Cyclone DDS, or alternative middleware.The DDS and RTPS Layer implements the OMG Data Distribution Service. It serializes structured ROS data into binary byte streams using Common Data Representation (CDR), tracks local entity state, and manages network sockets or shared memory segments.The Dynamic Discovery Mechanism (RTPS)ROS 2 eliminates the centralized master by utilizing the OMG Real-Time Publish-Subscribe (RTPS) protocol to discover peers dynamically over physical network interfaces. Discovery executes in two distinct phases:PhaseProtocolTransport & AddressingInformation ExchangedOutcomePhase 1: Participant DiscoverySimple Participant Discovery Protocol (SPDP).Periodic UDP Multicast (239.255.0.1) on calculated domain ports.DomainParticipant GUID, protocol version, vendor ID, unicast IP/port locators.Nodes establish mutual awareness and open reliable unicast channels.Phase 2: Endpoint DiscoverySimple Endpoint Discovery Protocol (SEDP).Reliable point-to-point UDP Unicast.Entity GUIDs, Topic names, data types, offered/requested QoS profiles.Endpoints matched; direct data path established between DataWriter and DataReader.The discovery mechanics follow a structured sequence:Multicast Port Binding: When a node process launches, its underlying DDS entity (the DomainParticipant) opens sockets bound to standardized multicast IP addresses and calculated port numbers. The port assignment is derived deterministically from the DDS Domain ID:
$$\text{Multicast Discovery Port} = 7400 + (250 \times \text{Domain\_ID})$$$$\text{Unicast Traffic Port} = 7411 + (250 \times \text{Domain\_ID}) + (2 \times \text{Participant\_ID})$$Periodic Announcement (SPDP): The participant periodically broadcasts SPDP announcement packets (DATA(p)) on the multicast discovery port. These packets announce the participant's Globally Unique Identifier (GUID) and its unicast locators. Any online participant listening on that port receives the packet and adds the new node to its internal directory.Endpoint Announcement (SEDP): Following participant discovery, both entities transition to reliable unicast communication to exchange endpoint descriptions via SEDP. Each participant transmits DATA(w) packets detailing its active DataWriters (publishers) and DATA(r) packets detailing its active DataReaders (subscribers). These packets specify the topic name, message type, and detailed QoS parameters.Compatibility Matching: Each participant runs a matching algorithm locally. An endpoint match occurs if and only if the topic names are identical, the message types match, and the offered QoS policies of the publisher equal or exceed the requested QoS policies of the subscriber. Once matched, application data flows directly between endpoints without intermediary routing.The ROS 2 Executor and Callback Scheduling MechanicsOnce data arrives over the network and is deserialized by the DDS layer, it must be scheduled for execution within the application process. In ROS 2, this scheduling is governed by the rclcpp Executor, which abstracts operating system thread dispatching.The execution pipeline operates through a repeating cycle:Wait-Set Construction: The Executor inspects all registered nodes, collecting handles for all active timers, DDS subscription message queues, service servers, and action clients.Operating System Polling: The wait-set is passed down to the OS kernel via non-blocking polling primitives (such as epoll or select), causing the executor thread to block until a timer expires, a network packet arrives, or an event triggers.Ready-Set Formulation: The Executor evaluates all entities marked ready by the OS, assembling a deterministic "ready-set" of executable callback functions.Non-Preemptive Sequential Dispatch: By default, the SingleThreadedExecutor iterates through the ready-set, invoking callbacks sequentially to completion. If a single callback blocks (e.g., executing a synchronous database lookup, sleeping, or running an expensive vision model), the executor cannot service other ready callbacks. All other sensor messages, watchdog timers, and health-check loops in that process are starved until the blocking callback returns.7. Because RTPS Simple Participant Discovery relies on periodic multicast announcements, every node across a multi-robot facility broadcasts packets to all machines on the subnet. On 802.11 Wi-Fi networks, multicast frames are transmitted at base mandatory rates (often 1 Mbps to 6 Mbps) without link-layer acknowledgements to ensure reception across diverse hardware. As the number of nodes $N$ grows across a fleet, discovery metatraffic expands quadratically ($O(N^2)$). The resulting broadcast traffic consumes available radio airtime, triggering widespread packet drops, high link jitter, and periodic disconnections across active data topics.A second catastrophic failure mode is the Synchronous Service Deadlock.     auto result = client_->async_send_request(request);

Because the single executor thread is blocked waiting for the service response, it is incapable of processing the incoming network packet that carries that response. The node process hangs indefinitely, fails external health checks, and must be terminated by process supervisors.A third subtle issue is Silent Quality of Service Incompatibility. DDS strictly enforces Offered versus Requested QoS contracts. If a sensor driver publishes a topic using BEST_EFFORT reliability while an analytics node subscribes requesting RELIABLE delivery, the DDS layer refuses to match the endpoints. The computational graph shows both nodes running and sharing the same topic name, yet no data flows, and no warning appears in standard console logs.Finally, Unbounded Callback Execution and Buffer Bloat degrade system responsiveness. If an image processing callback requires 50 ms to execute while the camera publishes frames every 33 ms (30 Hz), incoming messages accumulate in the subscription queue. Research & Emerging DirectionsAcademic and industrial research focuses on addressing the core scheduling and networking bottlenecks of distributed robotics middleware:Formal Response-Time Analysis of ROS 2 Processing ChainsIn safety-critical systems, developers must guarantee that the end-to-end latency from sensor detection to actuator output satisfies a strict deadline:$$L_{\text{end-to-end}} = R_{\text{sensor}} + \sum R_{\text{processing}} + R_{\text{actuator}} \le D_{\text{deadline}}$$
Casini et al. (ECRTS 2019) developed the first formal mathematical scheduling model for the ROS 2 executor. Their research proved that the default ROS 2 executor's callback dispatching induces structural priority inversion and unbounded callback latencies when multiple processing pipelines share execution threads. Under heavy subscriber fan-out, median processing chain latency increases from 0.23 ms with a single subscriber to 2.18 ms with 56 subscribers, while 99th-percentile tail latency expands from 0.70 ms to 13.23 ms. Subsequent research by Blaß et al. and Tang et al. established compositional performance analysis (CPA) frameworks showing that rate-priority executors can reduce 99th-percentile response times by 42.9% and eliminate up to 29.7% of deadline misses under concurrent workloads.Next-Generation Schedulers and Time-Sensitive NetworkingTo overcome default executor limitations, current research explores real-time deterministic architectures:Deterministic Callback Schedulers: Schedulers like the Bosch Prio-Executor replace flat wait-set polling with rate-monotonic priority queues, preventing low-rate background tasks from starving high-frequency control loops.Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN): The integration of DDS over IEEE 802.1Qbv TSN standards allows roboticists to map DDS QoS policies directly into hardware-scheduled Ethernet transmission queues. This enables microsecond-level deterministic data delivery across shared physical Ethernet backbones, bridging the gap between fieldbuses and high-level software stacks.Decentralized Consensus and Multi-Agent CoordinationIn multi-robot swarms, coordinating tasks without central servers remains a key research area. Operating over dynamic communication graphs $\mathcal{G}(t) = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}(t))$ where RF links form and break dynamically, researchers utilize the Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm (CBBA) for decentralized task allocation. CBBA iterates between local auction phases and consensus phases where robots resolve conflicting task assignments strictly through local peer-to-peer communication, guaranteeing conflict-free convergence even when network partitions split the swarm.11. Research GapsDespite the maturity of ROS 2 and DDS, several architectural challenges remain unresolved in both literature and industrial practice:First, Deterministic Multi-Threaded Executor Scheduling remains an open problem. Standard rclcpp executors cannot provide mathematically provable worst-case execution time (WCET) bounds across complex processing graphs without requiring custom scheduling patches. Developing a lightweight, standards-compliant real-time executor that prevents priority inversion while supporting dynamic graph changes remains an active area of investigation.Second, Managing the CAP Theorem during Network Partitioning presents persistent operational trade-offs in mobile robotics. When an autonomous mobile robot enters a Wi-Fi dead zone, it faces a fundamental choice between Consistency (fleet-wide map and task alignment) and Availability (continuing local navigation). Standardized protocols for cleanly merging conflicting topological maps and resolving distributed state after network reconnection are still evolving.Third, Microcontroller Resource Constraints continue to limit embedded DDS adoption. While micro-ROS brings DDS entities to microcontrollers, running native peer-to-peer DDS endpoints on constrained microcontrollers without intermediary proxy agents remains difficult due to dynamic memory requirements and discovery overhead.Finally, Cross-Vendor RMW Interoperability exhibits practical limitations. Although DDS and RTPS are open international standards, mixing nodes utilizing different RMW implementations (e.g., rmw_fastrtps_cpp communicating with rmw_cyclonedds_cpp) across complex QoS configurations often leads to subtle endpoint matching failures and serialization mismatches under high data loads.16.
